# Unit 4 — The Second Declension

Masculine and neuter nouns in -ος and -ον; cases, word order and movable ν.

## Summary

Greek nouns change their endings for case: nominative for the subject, genitive for "of", dative for "to" or "for", accusative for the direct object, vocative for address. Because the case shows the job of each word, Greek word order is free: ἄνθρωπος βλέπει δοῦλον and δοῦλον βλέπει ἄνθρωπος both mean "a man sees a servant".

## λόγος and δῶρον

| Case | Singular | Plural | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | λόγος | λόγοι | δῶρον | δῶρα |
| Genitive | λόγου | λόγων | δώρου | δώρων |
| Dative | λόγῳ | λόγοις | δώρῳ | δώροις |
| Accusative | λόγον | λόγους | δῶρον | δῶρα |
| Vocative | λόγε | λόγοι | δῶρον | δῶρα |

Neuter nouns have the same form in the nominative and accusative. There is no indefinite article: λόγος is "a word".

## Accent of nouns

A noun keeps its accent on the syllable where the nominative has it, as far as the general rules allow; in the genitive and dative an accented ultima takes the circumflex: θεός, θεοῦ, θεῷ.

## How the course checks word order

You may type a Greek answer in any order of the right words, as long as no postpositive word (δέ, γάρ, οὖν) stands first and no article stands last; when your order differs from ours the feedback shows ours.

## Common confusion

λόγοι (nominative plural) and λόγους (accusative plural) differ only in the ending: read to the end of every word.
