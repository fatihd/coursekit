# Unit 64 — Nouns from Double-Ayin Roots

Read nouns built on geminate (double-Ayin) roots.

## Summary

Biliteral nouns from geminate roots duplicate their second radical before an ending: עָם (people) gives עַמִּי (my people), לֵב (heart) gives לִבִּי (my heart). In their bare monosyllabic form the short vowels i and u lengthen to e and o — לֵב (heart), עֹז (strength) — while a usually stays. The doubled consonant reappears the moment a suffix or plural is added.

## Geminate nouns

Some nouns are built, like geminate verbs, on roots whose last two radicals are the same, so the root collapses to two letters that double before an ending: עָם (people), לֵב (heart), חֹק (statute), עֹז (strength). In the bare form the short vowels i and u have lengthened to e and o — לֵב, עֹז — while a usually remains: עָם, יָד (hand).

## Their inflection

Before suffixes and many plural endings the doubled radical is visible: עַמִּי (my people), לִבִּי (my heart), חֻקִּים (statutes), and עַמִּים (peoples). The doubled consonant identifies the class, while the vowel may change with the form — compare חֹק with חֻקִּים. Not every noun follows the same surface pattern, especially where a guttural or resh cannot take doubling; compare absolute, construct, and suffixed forms rather than generating every form mechanically.

## Common confusion

Do not confuse a geminate noun with a hollow noun or a strong segholate. Geminate nouns have identical second and third radicals, often visible as doubling when an ending is added; hollow nouns reflect a weak middle waw or yod; a segholate has a helping vowel between its last consonants. Recover the root from the inflected forms and compare the vowel pattern before deciding which family you have.
