# Unit 5 — Vowel Qualities and Vowel Letters

Compare pure-long, tone-long, and short vowels.

## Summary

Hebrew has three primary vowel qualities — a, i, and u — and two more, e and o. Each appears long or short. Length matters: a long vowel fills a syllable differently from a short one, and many noun and verb patterns depend on the difference. Learn the pairs — long ā and short a, long ē and short e, long î and short i, long ô and short o, long û and short u — and notice that the vowel letters ו and י usually accompany the long forms.

## Vowel qualities


| Quality | Long | Short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a | ā (qamets) | a (patah) |
| e | ē (tsere) | e (segol) |
| i | î (hiriq-yod) | i (hiriq) |
| o | ô (holem) | o (qamets-hatuf) |
| u | û (shureq) | u (qibbuts) |


## Vowel length

A long vowel is often written with a vowel letter to hold it, while a short vowel usually stands alone. Compare קָטַל (qāṭal): the first vowel is long ā under ק, the second is short a under ט. The pattern of long and short vowels is part of the word's identity, so changing length often changes meaning or form.

## Qamets and qamets-hatuf

The sign qamets (ָ) usually stands for long ā, but in a closed, unaccented syllable it is read as short o, called qamets-hatuf. The difference is governed by the syllable, so it is best learned together with syllable division: a qamets in a closed syllable that is not stressed is the short o.

## Common confusion

Do not assume one sign always means one sound in every position. Qamets is the clearest example: ā in one syllable, o in another. When you meet a new word, divide it into syllables first, then decide how a qamets is read.
