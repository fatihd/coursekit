# Unit 45 — The Verb Stems: An Overview

See the scheme of the derived stems and how they are named.

## Summary

Besides the simple stem, Qal, a Hebrew verb can appear in derived stems that add intensive, causative, reflexive, or passive force. The old grammarians named each stem after the form it takes in the model verb פָּעַל (to do): the intensive is the Piel, the causative the Hiphil, and so on. Learn the scheme once; the next units take each stem in turn.

## The scheme

|  | Simple | Intensive | Causative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| active | Qal קָטַל | Piel קִטֵּל | Hiphil הִקְטִיל |
| passive | — | Pual קֻטַּל | Hophal הָקְטַל |
| reflexive | Niphal נִקְטַל | Hitpael הִתְקַטֵּל | — |

## The names

Hebrew had no words like "intensive" or "causative", so it named each stem by the matching form of פָּעַל. The intensive active of פעל is פִּעֵל, so the intensive of any verb is called its Piel; the causative active is הִפְעִיל, the Hiphil. Only the simple stem is not named by its form: it is the Qal, "light". Because the ע of פעל is a guttural and cannot be doubled, the intensive's doubling does not show in it, so modern grammars set out the stems with קָטַל instead.

## Reading a stem

Each stem has its signs: Niphal a prefixed נ; Piel, Pual, and Hitpael a doubled middle root letter; Hiphil and Hophal a prefixed ה in the perfect; Hitpael the prefix הִתְ. Find the sign, then the root, then the meaning.

## Common confusion

Do not translate a stem by a fixed formula. Piel is "intensive" by name but often makes a verb transitive or causes a state; Niphal is often passive but sometimes reflexive. The names label forms; each verb's meaning in each stem must be learned.

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §24.
