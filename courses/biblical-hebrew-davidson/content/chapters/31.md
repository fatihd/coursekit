# Unit 31 — Higher Numbers and Ordinals

Count above ten and use the ordinals.

## Summary

Eleven to nineteen join a unit to ten, each in a masculine and a feminine form. The tens are plurals of the units; hundred and thousand are nouns with duals for two hundred and two thousand. The ordinals first to tenth are adjectives; above ten the cardinals serve as ordinals.

## Eleven to nineteen and the tens

Eleven through nineteen have masculine and feminine forms. They combine a unit with "ten": אַחַד עָשָׂר (eleven, masculine), אַחַת עֶשְׂרֵה (eleven, feminine); שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר (twelve, masculine), שְׁתֵּים עֶשְׂרֵה (twelve, feminine). The same gendered pattern continues through תִּשְׁעָה עָשָׂר / תְּשַׁע עֶשְׂרֵה (nineteen). These compound numbers are in apposition to the counted noun. The tens are the plurals of the units: שְׁלֹשָׁה (three) gives שְׁלֹשִׁים (thirty), חָמֵשׁ (five) gives חֲמִשִּׁים (fifty); twenty is עֶשְׂרִים, the plural of ten. The tens end in -îm for either gender; the form of the counted noun depends on whether the numeral precedes or follows it.

## Compound numbers and counted nouns

Numbers made from a ten and a unit may put the unit first or the ten first: שְׁלֹשָׁה וְעֶשְׂרִים and עֶשְׂרִים וְשְׁלֹשָׁה (twenty-three). With units two through ten the counted noun is usually plural and the numeral commonly stands before it in the construct. Tens generally take a singular noun when they precede it and a plural when they follow it. Eleven through nineteen usually take a plural noun, with some frequent nouns—such as day, year, and person—also appearing in the singular. Learn the construction with the counted noun, not as a number list alone.

## Hundreds, thousands, and ordinals

Hundred is מֵאָה (plural מֵאוֹת; dual מָאתַיִם, two hundred), and thousand is אֶלֶף (plural אֲלָפִים; dual אַלְפַּיִם). Higher hundreds combine a numeral with מֵאוֹת, for example שְׁלֹשׁ מֵאוֹת (three hundred); compound thousands follow the same additive pattern. The ordinals first through tenth are adjectives and agree with the noun in gender: רִאשׁוֹן / רִאשׁוֹנָה (first), שֵׁנִי / שְׁנִיָּה (second), שְׁלִישִׁי / שְׁלִישִׁית (third), and so on. Beyond ten the cardinal numbers serve also as ordinals.

## Common confusion

Davidson also notes that the feminine ordinal can name a fraction, as “a third.” This is a separate use of the same adjective pattern, not a special suffix meaning “fraction.” When a number is compounded, keep the counted noun and its agreement in view: the same numeral can occur before or after the noun, and the order can vary. The passage, not an assumed English word order, determines whether the phrase is counting objects, giving an age or date, or expressing an ordinal.
