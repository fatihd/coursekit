# Unit 39 — Wayyiqtol

Read the waw-consecutive imperfect in narrative sequences.

## Summary

After a perfect, the events that follow in a past narrative are told with the conjunction waw joined to the imperfect — the form traditionally called wayyiqtol ("and he did"). The waw is not a mere "and": it marks the next step in a sequence. Davidson warns against calling it "waw conversive" as if it converted one tense into another; the form and its place in the narrative carry the meaning, not a mechanical tense change.

## Wayyiqtol

After an initial perfect (or its equivalent) the following verbs of a past sequence take waw with the imperfect. The imperfect is pointed like the article, and the tone is usually drawn back toward the penult. Read the sequence as a chain of completed events, each linked to the one before it, and translate each as a simple past.

## Sequence rather than conversion

Davidson rejects the old explanation that waw “converts” an imperfect into a past tense. The conjunction has a subordinating or consecutive force: it presents the next event as following from the one before. The initial perfect establishes a completed event; the closely connected imperfects continue the resulting narrative sequence. Translation into English past tense is often right in prose, but the Hebrew form itself has not changed its basic imperfect character.

The first perfect need not always be the immediately preceding written verb. A clause or expression can imply the completed event that supplies the starting point. Read the surrounding sentence before deciding what the chain continues. Conversely, if the connection between waw and the verb is broken by an intervening word, Davidson says the ordinary tense construction returns; do not label every waw followed somewhere by an imperfect as wayyiqtol.

## Form clues

The consecutive waw is pointed like the article and is followed by a doubled initial consonant where the consonant permits it. The imperfect's tone commonly retreats from the last syllable to the penult. These signs work together: look for the waw attached directly to an imperfect, then check the consonant and accent pattern. The doubled consonant is a form clue, while the preceding narrative context is the meaning clue.

## Recognizing it

The waw of the wayyiqtol is written with a following dagesh and a patah (like the article), and the verb is an imperfect whose accent has shifted back. In a narrative the first verb is often a perfect, and all the rest are wayyiqtol. If any other word comes between the waw and the verb, the construction is broken and the ordinary tense returns.

## Common confusion

Do not treat wayyiqtol as "waw + future". The imperfect here continues a completed, past sequence because the waw subordinates it to what precedes. Translate the chain clause by clause in order, not as a list of independent futures.

The construction may also follow an implied perfect, and a wayyiqtol can introduce a narrative sequence after a temporal phrase or other completed event. Parse the relation between clauses first; use the chain's discourse role to choose an English tense. The form should not be translated mechanically without its opening context.

## Source

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §23.
