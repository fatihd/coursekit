# Unit 11 — Noun Patterns and Plurals

Recognize the segholate, strong, and participial noun families and their plurals.

## Summary

Hebrew nouns fall into a few pattern families, distinguished by how their vowels shift when a plural or construct ending is added. The segholates (מֶלֶךְ, king; סֵפֶר, book) are really monosyllables with a helping vowel slipped in; the strong nouns (דָּבָר, word) resemble the verb perfect; and the participial nouns (כֹּהֵן, priest; אֹיֵב, enemy) end in -ē with an unchangeable vowel. Each family changes its vowels differently under inflection, and recognizing the family is the key to reading the plural and construct forms correctly.

## Three pattern families

Nouns may be arranged by the internal vowel changes produced when the place of the tone shifts through inflection. Words with unchangeable vowels — pure-long or diphthongal — suffer no internal change and need no classification; but the forms with changeable vowels fall into a few clear families. Three of them cover most nouns: the segholates, the strong (verb-like) nouns, and the participial nouns.

## The segholates

A segholate looks like a two-syllable word accented on the first syllable (מֶלֶךְ, king; סֵפֶר, book; קֹדֶשׁ, holiness). It is really a monosyllable — malk, siphr, qodhsh — into which a furtive vowel (seghol) has been slipped to separate the final two consonants, which Hebrew will not pronounce together. With an inflectional addition the word returns to its monosyllabic core: מַלְכִּי (my king), סִפְרוֹ (his book). The plural takes a helping a: מְלָכִים (kings), סְפָרִים (books).

## The strong (verb-like) nouns

A large class of nouns has the form of the verb perfect and is chiefly made of participles and adjectives, though many are substantives: דָּבָר (word), חָכָם (wise), קָדוֹשׁ (holy). Their vowels are tone-long and therefore changeable. When the plural ending is added, the tone plants itself on the ending, and the original vowel, now two places from the tone, reduces to shewa: דְּבָרִים (words). The construct is made as short as possible.

## The participial nouns

A third family ends in -ē with an unchangeable vowel before it: כֹּהֵן (priest), אֹיֵב (enemy), שֹׁפֵט (judge). These follow the verb's law of inflection: with a vocalic suffix the tone-vowel reduces to shewa (כֹּהֲנֵי, priests of), and with a consonantal suffix it shortens. Many words formed with a prefixed m — מִשְׁפָּט (judgment), מַלְאָךְ (messenger) — belong to this family.

## Common confusion

Do not try to memorize a list of numbered "declensions" as rigid categories. Learn the families and how each behaves under the plural and construct. The segholate's helping vowel disappears before a suffix; the strong noun reduces its second vowel; the participial noun keeps its -ē. Read the form, identify the family, and the inflection follows.
