# Unit 53 — Weak Verbs: Names and Classes

Name a weak verb by the position of its weak letter.

## Summary

A weak verb has a weak letter among its three root letters: a guttural (א, ה, ח, ע, and for some purposes ר), a quiescent (א, ה, ו, י), or נ. Weak verbs are named by the position of the weak letter, using the letters of the model verb פָּעַל: פ for the first root letter, ע for the second, ל for the third.

## Naming by פ, ע, ל

נָפַל (fall) is a Pe-Nun verb, its first letter נ; יָשַׁב (sit) is Pe-Yod; קוּם (rise) is Ayin-Waw, or hollow; שָׁמַע (hear) is Lamed-Guttural; גָּלָה (uncover) is Lamed-He. א and ה are gutturals at the start of a root and quiescents at the end, so הָלַךְ (go) is Pe-Guttural but גָּלָה Lamed-He, and אָסַף (gather) is Pe-Guttural but מָצָא (find) Lamed-Aleph. A few verbs whose א falls silent in the imperfect, like אָמַר (יֹאמַר), are called Pe-Aleph. A verb whose second and third letters are the same, like סָבַב (go around), is Double-Ayin.

## More than one weak letter

A verb with two weak letters is named after both: יָדָה (throw, praise) is Pe-Yod and Lamed-He. Each weak letter follows its own rule.

## Not irregular

"Irregular" is a misnomer. The weak verbs follow the regular paradigm, changed in predictable ways by their weak letter. A Piel cannot double a guttural middle letter, so בֵּרַךְ (he blessed) lengthens the vowel before the ר instead of doubling it. Learn the rules for gutturals, quiescents, and נ, and the forms follow.

## Common confusion

Do not memorize weak verbs as exceptions. Name the root's weak letters first, then apply each one's rule: a missing letter (יִפֹּל from נפל) or a changed vowel (בֵּרַךְ) is the rule at work.

## Source

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §32.
