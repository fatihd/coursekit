# Unit 34 — Adverbs and Interjections

Read adverbs, interjections, and oath formulas.

## Summary

Most particles are nouns, whole or worn down, put to a new use. Many adverbs are nouns with an old ending ־ָם: יוֹמָם (by day), חִנָּם (for nothing). The interjections are few: הִנֵּה (behold), הוֹי and אוֹי (ah!, alas!). In oaths אִם means "surely not" and אִם־לֹא "surely".

## The nature of particles

Particles are mostly nouns, whole or worn down. Being nouns, they stand in the construct state when they govern a following word, which is then in the genitive — though Hebrew does not mark case endings, so this is more a way of thinking about them than a visible form. Some particles are so feeble that they cannot stand alone but prefix themselves to a noun or pronoun; others are inseparable affixes.

This description is a useful way to understand why many particles form tight units with what follows. Some survive as independent words; others are fragments that can no longer stand alone. Several take the same kinds of pronominal suffixes found on nouns and prepositions. The suffix expresses the participant related to that particle, while the particle supplies the relation. Learn the whole attached form in context rather than trying to translate each letter separately.

## Adverbs and interjections

Many adverbs are directly connected with nouns and end in -ām, an old accusative ending: יוֹמָם (by day), חִנָּם (in vain). Others are the noun סָבִיב (circuit), used as "round about". The interjections are few but vivid: הוֹי and אוֹי (ah!, alas!), הִנֵּה (behold), and הֶאָח (ha!). In oaths, אִם means "certainly not" and אִם־לֹא "certainly".

The same surface form can belong to different grammatical uses. An adverb may have grown from a noun, a preposition may be extended into a conjunction, and an interjection may stand alone as an emotional response. Identify what the word connects to: a noun phrase, a clause, a question, or no syntactic partner. In an oath, אִם / אִם־לֹא is idiomatic and does not have its ordinary “if / if not” force; Davidson explains it as an abbreviated conditional oath. Translate the whole formula, not its pieces word for word.

## Common confusion

Do not translate הִנֵּה as a verb: it points to what follows ("behold", "look", or simply "now"). And read an oath formula as a whole: אִם in an oath is not "if".

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §49.
