# Unit 9 — The Quiescent Letters

Recognize when א, ה, ו, and י fall silent.

## Summary

The letters א, ה, ו, and י are weak. At the start of a syllable they are consonants; at the end of a syllable after a vowel they usually lose their consonant sound and fall silent, or quiesce. The syllable then ends in a vowel, and that vowel is usually long. Much of the behavior of the weak verbs follows from this.

## Consonant or silent

Compare אָמַר (ʾāmar, he said), where א opens the syllable and is sounded, with יֹאמַר (yōmar, he says), where א closes the first syllable and is silent. A silent letter takes no shewa: בִּימֵי (in the days of), not בִּיְמֵי; רֵאשִׁית (beginning), with no shewa under its silent א. If such a letter does carry shewa, it is being treated as a consonant.

## Silent aleph lengthens the vowel

At the end of a word א is always silent, and usually at the end of a syllable. The syllable is then as good as open, so its vowel is long: on the pattern of קָטַל one would expect מָצַא, but the word is מָצָא (he found). In מָצָאתָ (you found) the ת has no dagesh lene, because the silent א leaves a vowel before it.

## Final ו and י

At the end of a word ו and י are vowel letters when the vowel before them matches: ו after o or u, י after i or e: לִי (to me), לוֹ (to him). After a different vowel they are consonants: חַי (ḥay, living), הוֹי (hôy, ah!), יַחְדָּו (yaḥdāw, together). The ending ־ָיו is read āw: סוּסָיו (his horses).

## Common confusion

Do not drop a quiescent letter when you look up a word. It has fallen silent, not disappeared: יֹאמַר still comes from אָמַר, and מָצָא still has the root מצא.

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §9.
