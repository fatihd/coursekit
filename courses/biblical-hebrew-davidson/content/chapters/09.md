# Unit 9 — Roots and Patterns

Use consonantal roots and patterns to analyze vocabulary.

## Summary

Most Hebrew words are built from a root of (usually) three consonants placed into a pattern of vowels and affixes. The root carries the core meaning; the pattern carries the form and the grammatical job. Recognising the root lets you group related words — מֶלֶךְ (king), מַלְכָּה (queen), מַמְלָכָה (kingdom) all share מ־ל־ך — and to make a first guess at a new word's meaning.

## Root and pattern


| Root | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| מ־ל־ך | to rule | מֶלֶךְ (king), מַלְכָּה (queen), מַמְלָכָה (kingdom) |
| ס־פ־ר | to count, recount | סֵפֶר (book), סָפַר (he counted) |
| ק־ד־שׁ | to be holy | קָדוֹשׁ (holy), קֹדֶשׁ (holiness) |


## The root

A root is the three consonants that persist across a family of words. The root מ־ל־ך means "to rule"; ק־ד־שׁ means "to be holy"; ס־פ־ר means "to count or recount". The root is an abstraction: it is not a word by itself, but it is the meaning shared by every word built on it. Write it with hyphens (or a dot) to show you mean the root, not a word.

## Patterns

A pattern (mishqal) is a vowel-and-affix frame into which a root is set. The same root in different patterns gives different nouns and verbs. The pattern tells you the word class and often a shade of meaning, while the root tells you the basic idea. Learning a few common patterns makes new vocabulary much easier.

## Common confusion

Do not confuse a root with a word, and do not force a meaning onto every word with the same root. Roots are a reading aid, not a translation machine; context and the dictionary still decide the exact sense. Also, weak roots (with ו, י, or a repeated letter) change their letters across forms, so a root may be hidden.

## Source

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (1914), §16, §20.1.
