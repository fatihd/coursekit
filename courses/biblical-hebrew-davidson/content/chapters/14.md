# Unit 14 — Adjective Comparison

Form comparatives and superlatives with מִן, the article, and repetition.

## Summary

The Hebrew adjective undergoes no change of ending or vowels in comparison; there are no forms like "better" or "best". The comparative is expressed by the positive adjective followed by the preposition מִן (from): טוֹב מִיַּיִן, "good away from wine", that is "better than wine". The superlative is expressed by isolating the adjective — with the article, a suffix, or a construct relation — or by intensifying it with "very" or by repetition.

## The comparative

To say "better than wine" Hebrew writes טוֹב מִיַּיִן, literally "good from wine", or "good in distinction from wine". The preposition מִן does the whole work of "than". The same construction is used with verbs: אֶגְדַּל מִמְּךָ, "I will be greater than you". Let מִן before the standard of comparison be the sign to translate "than".

## The superlative

The superlative is also expressed by the positive adjective raised into isolation. "He is the greatest" is written הוּא הַגָּדוֹל, literally "he, the great one", with the article marking the one set apart. A suffix or a construct relation does the same: קְטֹן בָּנָיו, "the youngest of his sons". This is the "great one among" idea: the adjective is singled out as the one who surpasses the rest.

## The absolute superlative

Absolute superlativeness — "most holy", "very good" — is expressed variously: by the word מְאֹד (very), as טוֹב מְאֹד (good exceedingly); or by repetition, as קֹדֶשׁ קָדָשִׁים, "holy of holies", that is "most holy". Repetition is a frequent and vivid way of saying "the very best, the most".

## Common confusion

Do not look for comparative or superlative endings on the adjective; Hebrew has none. Read מִן as "than", read the article or construct as "the … one of" or "the most", and read repetition as "most". The adjective's form stays the same throughout; only the construction changes the degree.

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §47.
