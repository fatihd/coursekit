# Unit 46 — Apocopated, Doubly Weak, and Defective Forms

Handle shortened and doubly weak verb forms.

## Summary

A few verb forms are shortened (apocopated), and some verbs combine two weak letters or are defective — lacking a form altogether. These are the remaining irregularities, and they follow from the weak-letter rules already learned, so they are best handled as recognitions rather than as a new memory load.

## Apocopated forms

Apocopation drops a final letter or vowel, especially in the jussive and before waw. The shortened form is the point of contact with the jussive and the waw-consecutive, where an open final syllable is cut back.

## Doubly weak verbs

A verb may share the peculiarities of two weak classes — a Pe-Yod and Lamed-He verb, for example. Such a verb is named after all the classes whose features it shows, and each weak letter is treated by its own rule. Read the two weak letters in turn, and the form becomes regular.

## Defective verbs

Some verbs are defective: they lack one or more of the expected forms entirely. A particular stem or conjugation may simply not occur. Recognise the gap rather than forcing a form that does not exist.

## Common confusion

Do not panic at a short or unexpected form. Apocopation and double weakness are regular outcomes of the weak-letter rules, and a missing form is a fact about that verb, not an error in the text. Recover the root, name the weak classes, and the form is intelligible.

## Worked example

A shortened jussive such as יְהִי ("let it be") drops its final vowel before waw or in the jussive. Recognise the short form by its missing ending, and recover the full form from context. Doubly weak verbs simply apply two weak-letter rules at once.

## Reading practice

Read and parse. וַיְהִי — "and it came to pass" (apocopated imperfect of היה with waw). The shortened form drops its final vowel; recover the full form from context and from the weak-letter rules.

Davidson, *An Introductory Hebrew Grammar* (22nd ed., 1923), §45.
